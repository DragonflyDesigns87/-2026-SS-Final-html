Unit 1 · Ch. 1, 2

What is a Nation?

To what extent should nation be the foundation of identity?

The 4 P's of a nation ★ exam likely
People
A shared group
Ethnic or civic identity
Place
A territory
Physical homeland
Principles
Shared laws & ideas
Constitution, rights
Patriotism
Love of nation
Emotional attachment
Place alone is never enough to make a nation. You need people or principles. That's why Antarctica isn't a nation — it has place, but no people or principles to go with it.
Civic vs ethnic nationalism
Civic nationalism
Based on shared principles turned into laws. Allegiance is to the state and its institutions. Canada, France, Britain, USA are civic nations. Anyone who accepts the laws can belong.
Ethnic nationalism
Based on shared ethnicity, language, ancestry, culture. Allegiance is to the people and traditions. Germany (historical), Korea, Japan are ethnic nations. Québec nationalism has strong ethnic elements.
Key difference
Civic: "citizen" is a legal status open to all. Ethnic: membership tied to birth and heritage. Both can coexist in one country.
8 common bonds (roots of a nation)
Language
Religion
Ethnicity / race
Shared history
Shared culture
Geography / territory
Common economic interests
Shared political system
Most tested: civic bonds (principles → laws) and ethnic bonds (people/ancestry). The other six support these two.
Nation vs nation-state
Nation
A people with a shared identity, culture, history — does not require a government or fixed territory
Nation-state
A political unit (country) where the people and the government largely align. Most countries today are nation-states, but many contain multiple nations (e.g., Canada includes Québécois, First Nations, and other groups)
Benedict Anderson
Called a nation an "imagined political community" — people feel connected to millions they'll never meet through shared symbols and stories
Collective consciousness
Definition
A shared internal sense of "we" — the common identity, values, and memory that unite a group. William Bloom: people internalize national symbols and can "act as one psychological group" when those symbols are threatened.
National myths
Shared stories (accurate or not) that connect people to the nation's past and shape how they see the world. Stories of heroism, struggle, hardship. Canada's myths: the "rugged outdoorsman," peacekeepers, hockey, coureurs de bois, building the CPR. Myths are not lies — they are "inspiring narratives in which we tell ourselves who we are or want to be."
Symbols
National flags, anthems, animals (beaver = Canada), coins, monuments. Symbols and myths together build collective identity. The maple leaf, the Mountie, the loon — all encode Canadian values.
Evolution vs revolution
Revolution
Sudden, dramatic overthrow — American Revolution ("life, liberty, pursuit of happiness"). Change happens first, then rules follow.
Evolution
Gradual, step-by-step change — the Canadian approach. Vote first, discuss, move slowly. Even Québec sovereignists want a referendum before separating.
Unit 1 · Ch. 3, 13, 14

Identity & Contending Loyalties

How do loyalties shape people's choices and create conflict?

Types of identity
Individual identity
"I" statements — personal values, beliefs, preferences. Unique to each person.
Collective identity
"We" statements — shared with a group (religion, culture, ethnicity). "In my Hindu community, we…"
National identity
Shared with the nation — internalized national symbols. "As Canadians, we should…"
Contending loyalties ★ key concept
Definition
Competing loyalties that pull people in different directions — loyalty to nation vs loyalty to religion, region, class, culture, etc.
Nationalist loyalty
Groups striving for sovereignty or political independence — e.g. Québec sovereignists, Tibet, Kosovo Albanians. Their goal is self-determination leading to a separate state.
Non-nationalist loyalty
Feeling underrepresented or marginalized but NOT seeking independence — e.g. Alberta regionalism, religious groups, class loyalty. They compete with national loyalty but don't aim for statehood.
The key test
Striving for sovereignty/independence? → Nationalist loyalty. Just feeling underrepresented? → Non-nationalist loyalty. This can be fluid — Alberta regionalism could slide into nationalism.
Types of non-nationalist loyalty: class, religious, regional, ideological, cultural, racial/ethnic. Any of these can compete with national loyalty.
Multiculturalism & reasonable accommodation
Cultural pluralism
Encouraging different cultural groups to maintain their identities within one society. Canada's "mosaic" vs USA's "melting pot."
Multiculturalism
Canada's official version of cultural pluralism. Policy since 1971, enhanced in the Canadian Multiculturalism Act (1988). Canada was the first country to adopt multiculturalism as official government policy.
Reasonable accommodation
Legal/constitutional requirement for public institutions to adapt to religious and cultural practices of minorities — as long as these don't violate other rights. Example: RCMP Sikh turban (Baltej Singh Dhillon, 1990) — allowed as a reasonable accommodation.
Debate
Some argue multiculturalism strengthens Canada (unity in diversity). Others argue it divides Canadians and weakens a shared nationalist loyalty. Québec debates reasonable accommodation around its Francophone identity.
Oka Crisis (1990) case study
What happened
Mohawks on the Kanesatake reserve near Oka, Québec, blocked expansion of a golf course onto sacred land they claimed as their own. 4-month standoff. Québec police moved in, shots fired, an officer was killed. Army called in with 2500 soldiers.
Outcome
Protest ended September 26. Most protesters found not guilty. Federal government later bought the land and gave it to the Mohawks. Left a legacy of bitterness.
Why it matters
Classic example of contending nationalist loyalties — Aboriginal self-determination vs provincial and federal authority. Led directly to the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples (1991).
Unit 2 — Heaviest · Ch. 5

National Interest & Foreign Policy

To what extent should national interest be pursued?

Teacher: Unit 2 is the heaviest unit on the final. Bank on it.
Key definitions
National interest
What a nation needs (necessities) or wants (desires) — just like personal needs vs wants, but for a whole country. Needs must be met; wants expand power and prosperity.
Domestic policy
Guides decisions within the country — e.g. changing laws, settling land claims, spending tax revenue
Foreign policy
Guides decisions about official relations with other countries — treaties, trade, human rights, UN involvement. Also called external relations or foreign affairs.
Stakeholders
Anyone who affects or is affected by the pursuit of national interest — allies, enemies, domestic groups. Achieving one nation's interest often means another doesn't get what it wants.
Nationalism vs internationalism
Nationalism = competitive — puts your nation first. Internationalism = cooperative — works with others for shared goals.
The cycle: nationalism → national interest → foreign policy
  • Domestic and international events awaken nationalist feelings in citizens
  • These feelings cause citizens to revise what they see as the national interest
  • Governments alter their foreign policies in response to these changing priorities
  • The new foreign policy then affects both domestic and international events — and the cycle continues
World War I & Its Consequences
WWI causes — nationalism & national interest
Root causes
European nations believed expanding territory and colonial possessions was in their national interest. This drove them to form alliances — and the alliance system dragged everyone into war when one country was threatened.
Spark
Gavrilo Princip (Serbian nationalist) assassinated Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria-Hungary — triggering alliance obligations across Europe
Canada's role
Before WWI, Britain controlled Canada's foreign policy — so when Britain declared war, Canada was automatically at war. After Canada's contribution, PM Robert Borden demanded the right to attend the Paris Peace Conference as an independent country.
Treaty of Versailles (1919) ★ know this
What Germany lost
Required to: reduce military, pay $30 billion in reparations, give up European territories and colonies, accept full responsibility ("war guilt clause") for causing the war
Why it mattered
Created massive resentment in Germany — economic devastation, inflation, humiliation. This resentment became fertile ground for Hitler and the Nazi Party.
Woodrow Wilson
US President proposed Fourteen Points — a fair peace with no reparations. Most were ignored by France and Britain. Also proposed the League of Nations. The US Senate refused to ratify the treaty, so the US never joined the League.
League of Nations
Created after WWI to maintain collective security. Failed — Japan invaded Manchuria (1931), Italy invaded Ethiopia (1935), Germany violated treaty terms. Members couldn't agree on military action. Replaced by the UN after WWII.
Ferdinand Foch (WWI Allied commander) said of the Treaty: "This is not a peace treaty. It is an armistice of 20 years." He was right — WWII began exactly 20 years later.
Middle East consequences of WWI
The betrayal
Arabs fought alongside the Allies against the Ottoman Turks, having been promised an independent homeland. Britain and France secretly agreed instead to divide the Middle East between themselves (Sykes-Picot Agreement). Arabs were not given their promised state.
Long-term impact
The broken promises and foreign-drawn borders created ongoing nationalist tensions in the Middle East that persist to this day. Demonstrates that national interests pursued without considering others create lasting problems.
Unit 2 · Ch. 6

Ultranationalism

To what extent can nationalism lead to ultranationalism?

What is ultranationalism? ★ core concept
Definition
An extreme, fanatical form of nationalism — intolerant of others, single-minded, willing to use violence to achieve its goals
3 warning signs
1. Violence against a targeted group   2. Targeted — aimed at one specific group   3. Fanatical — unwavering, won't back down or admit wrongdoing
Escalation path
Contempt/prejudice → Segregation → Rights denied → Deportation → Genocide. Each step dehumanizes the target group further, making the next step easier.
How ultranationalism develops
  • Economic/social crisis: The Great Depression of the 1930s created unemployment, poverty, and desperation. People looked for a strong leader who could fix things.
  • Charismatic authoritarian leader: Hitler, Stalin, Mussolini, Tojo — leaders who inspired devotion and silenced opposition with force
  • National myths of superiority: Propaganda promoting the idea that one group is superior and others are inferior or dangerous
  • Propaganda: Uses fear, insecurity, hatred — calls opponents names, hides defeats, appeals to religious/national symbols, blames problems on a scapegoat group
Nazi Germany (Hitler)
Rise to power
Post-WWI Germany: extreme inflation (money became nearly worthless), Great Depression, unemployment, humiliation from Treaty of Versailles. Hitler elected 1933, then dissolved parliament and declared himself dictator.
Ultranationalist policies
Rejected Treaty of Versailles; rebuilt army; declared "Aryan race" superior; stripped Jews of rights, property, education, jobs; Kristallnacht (1938) — night of anti-Jewish violence; books burned; media controlled by Joseph Goebbels
Propaganda tools
Radio, movies, giant rallies (up to 100,000), posters, pamphlets, carefully controlled media. "Today Germany, tomorrow the whole world."
Stalinist Soviet Union
Stalin's rule
Communist dictator from late 1920s to 1953. Wanted to replace loyalties of 100 distinct national groups with Soviet nationalism. Confiscated farmland, created collective farms — those who objected were executed or sent to forced-labour camps in Siberia.
Ukraine (Holodomor)
Ukrainian farmers refused to give up their land. Stalin confiscated their crops. Up to 10 million Ukrainians starved to death. Ukrainian language was outlawed. Ukrainians were the largest group of political prisoners in labour camps.
Scale
Between 20 and 60 million people executed or died in famines, forced-labour camps, and deportations under Stalin's rule
Imperial Japan (Tojo Hideki)
Rise of militarism
Ultranationalists rid Japan of democracy, making it a one-party military state. Emperor Hirohito revered as "Son of Heaven" but military leaders held real power. General Tojo became PM in 1941.
Ultranationalist values
Worship of the emperor as a demigod; belief Japanese people are superior with a heaven-ordained mission to expand; contempt for death; blind obedience. Education system based on "The Way of Subjects" — promoted military values, scorned Western ideas.
Military expansion
Invaded China, captured territory across Asia for raw materials and markets. Attacked Pearl Harbor (1941), bringing the US into WWII. Kamikaze pilots — suicide missions seen as ultimate expression of devotion to nation.
Appeasement — responding to ultranationalism
Appeasement
Giving in to an aggressor's demands hoping to avoid war. The Allies (Britain, France, Italy) gave Hitler the Sudetenland in 1938 in exchange for his promise not to expand further. Churchill called it choosing "dishonour" — and they still got war.
Why it failed
Hitler took the rest of Czechoslovakia in 1939 anyway. Appeasement emboldened aggression. Churchill was proven right — appeasing ultranationalists only encourages them.
Churchill: "An appeaser is someone who feeds a crocodile — hoping it will eat him last." Appeasement ultimately leads to war, just later and on worse terms.
3 responses to ultranationalism
Appeasement
Give in to demands hoping to avoid conflict. Delay tactic — ultimately failed against Hitler.
Internationalism
Build alliances, strengthen collective numbers, use cooperative diplomatic and economic pressure. The long-term productive response.
Confrontation / war
Direct military opposition. Ultimately what stopped Nazi Germany, Imperial Japan, and Fascist Italy.
Unit 2 · Ch. 7

Crimes Against Humanity

To what extent can the pursuit of ultranationalism lead to crimes against humanity?

Legal definitions ★ know precisely
Genocide
Killing members of a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group; causing serious bodily or mental harm; deliberately destroying conditions of life to bring about the group's physical destruction
Crimes against humanity
Widespread or systematic attacks against a civilian population — murder, extermination, enslavement, deportation, imprisonment, torture, rape or sexual slavery, enforced disappearance, the crime of apartheid
War crimes
Wilful killing, torture, or inhuman treatment; wilfully causing great suffering; intentionally directing attacks against the civilian population or against those involved in a humanitarian or peacekeeping mission
Ethnic cleansing
Forcing a particular ethnic group out of a territory through violence, intimidation, or expulsion — making an area "ethnically pure"
Case studies — perpetrators vs victims ★ know these pairings
You won't be asked for dates or individual names — but you MUST correctly identify which group were perpetrators and which were victims in each case.
Perpetrators: Ottoman Turks
Armenian Genocide (WWI era)
~1.5 million Armenians killed. Turkey's official state policy still denies it occurred. Turkish journalist Hrant Dink was killed for reporting on it.
Perpetrators: Nazi Germany
The Holocaust
6 million Jews killed + Roma, disabled, Slavs, homosexuals. Auschwitz-Birkenau death camp. Systematic — rights stripped first, then deportation, then extermination.
Perpetrators: Stalinist USSR
Holodomor (Ukraine)
~3–10 million Ukrainians deliberately starved. Crops confiscated, language outlawed. Largest group in Soviet labour camps.
Perpetrators: Hutu ultranationalists
Rwanda Genocide (1994)
~800,000 Tutsis killed in 100 days. Radio propaganda urged killing. Identity cards (introduced by Belgian colonizers) used to identify victims. Gen. Roméo Dallaire (Canadian) commanded 2,600 UN peacekeepers — his warnings ignored, requests denied. Led to ICC creation.
Perpetrators: Bosnian Serbs
Bosnia/Sarajevo (1990s)
Ethnic cleansing of Bosnian Muslims in the former Yugoslavia. UN peacekeepers present but powerless. Led to peacekeeping reform debate.
International Criminal Court (ICC)
Created
Conceived by the UN in 1998. Permanent court located in The Hague, Netherlands. Supported by Canada. Tries people for genocide, crimes against humanity, and war crimes.
What it does
Holds individuals — not just states — accountable for "the most serious crimes of international concern." Nations that support it believe peace and security are in everyone's national interest.
Limitations
Needs state cooperation to function. Poor deterrent — only deals with crimes after they occur, and only when cooperating states hand over suspects. Does not prevent genocide from happening.
Canadian examples of ultranationalist tendencies
Japanese internment (WWII)
Rights stripped, property confiscated, Japanese Canadians locked up. Targeted one group in a hateful way — but Canada eventually admitted it was wrong and retracted. Not fully ultranationalist because it wasn't fanatically maintained.
Conscription (WWI & WWII)
Forced military service stripped freedoms and deeply divided French vs English Canada. The government even held a plebiscite in 1942 to break PM King's promise not to send conscripts overseas — 79% of anglophones voted yes, 85% of Francophones voted no.
These examples show Canada coming close to the line — but not crossing into true ultranationalism because there was resistance, debate, and eventual rejection of these policies.
Unit 2 · Ch. 8

Self-Determination

To what extent should national self-determination be pursued?

Sovereignty vs self-determination ★ tricky distinction
Sovereignty
Full political independence — being in charge of your own state, free from outside control. The highest level of political authority. If a source mentions "sovereignty," read it as national independence. Sovereignty always stands above self-determination.
Self-determination
The right of a people to freely choose how they'll be governed. Does not always mean full independence — can include self-government within a larger state.
First Nations example
First Nations want self-determination (the right to self-government within Canada) — NOT sovereignty. Full sovereignty would mean they're in charge of all of Canada, which isn't the goal. This is their home — they want control of their own affairs, not a separate state.
Source tip: If a source shows First Nations asking for "sovereignty," that's actually misleading phrasing — they want self-determination. If a source shows Québec sovereignists, they want full political independence from Canada — that IS sovereignty.
Québec sovereignty debate case study
1995 referendum
NO: 50.5% — YES: 49.42%. Razor-thin margin. 93.5% of Québécois voted. Described as a "near-death experience" for Canadian federalists.
Sovereignists
Québécois must control their own destiny (Lucien Bouchard: "We are a people, a nation, and as a nation we have a fundamental right to our territory"). Support Parti Québécois (PQ).
Federalists
Québécois identity is inseparable from Canadian identity. Canada was born in 1534, not 1867 — Québécois history is Canada's history.
Quiet Revolution
Transformed Québec from rural/religious to modern/urban. Bill 101 (1977) protected French as the language of government, workplaces, education, and business. Raised Francophone economic status but some became less interested in sovereignty once they were prospering within Canada.
2006 — Harper motion
"The Québécois form a nation within a united Canada." Passed House of Commons 266–16. Nation within Canada, not independence.
Kosovo & Tibet — international self-determination
Kosovo
Ethnic Albanians (~90%) vs ethnic Serbs. Kosovo declared independence from Serbia in 2008. UN forces administered the province after 1990s conflict. Russia and China called the declaration "null and void" — conflict between state sovereignty and peoples' self-determination.
Tibet
China invaded in 1950, suppressed Buddhist religion, destroyed monasteries. Tibetans pursue self-determination — Dalai Lama appealed to UN. Chinese resettlement program (Qinghai–Tibet Railway, 2006) brought Chinese immigrants, threatening Tibetan majority status. 3 UN General Assembly resolutions supported Tibetan self-determination.
First Nations self-determination in Canada
What they want
Self-government within Canada — right to determine political status, pursue economic/social/cultural development, manage their own resources. Not independence, not sovereignty — self-government.
Royal Commission (1996)
People to People, Nation to Nation — 66 recommendations. Main conclusion: 150+ years of assimilation policy "was wrong." Urged Canada to see First Nations as nations with the right to self-government in partnership with Canada.
Statement of Reconciliation (1998)
Federal government expressed regret for suppressing Aboriginal culture. AFN gave federal government a failing grade in 2006 — most recommendations not implemented. 800+ specific land claims still unresolved.
Nisga'a Agreement (1998)
Landmark: 2000 km² of traditional territory in BC + right to self-government over culture, language, land use, health, education, mineral resources. Model for future agreements.
Nunavut (1999)
New territory created April 1, 1999. 85% Inuit population. Gradually took over own administration. Traditional Inuit knowledge (quajimajatuqangit) incorporated into government policies.
Unit 3 · Ch. 9, 11, 12

Internationalism & Global Institutions

To what extent should internationalism be pursued?

Why internationalism emerged
  • Two World Wars proved that national competition leads to catastrophic destruction
  • The League of Nations (created after WWI) failed to maintain peace — Japan, Italy, Germany all violated agreements with no real consequences
  • Countries decided the only hope was joining together as a world community with collective responsibility
  • The United Nations was founded after WWII with 192 member countries
Key international institutions
United Nations
Peace, security, human rights. Security Council = most powerful decision-making body. All members agree to keep forces available for the Security Council. Provides a forum for disputes. Has not prevented all armed conflict, but no third world war.
World Bank / IMF
Economic stability — originally rebuilt post-war Europe, now assist developing countries. Criticized for being dominated by Western nations (US, Europe) whose policies sometimes harm developing countries.
WTO
Evolved from GATT (1947) — governs international trade rules, streamlines trade, resolves trade disputes. Also criticized for favouring wealthy nations.
ICC
International Criminal Court — tries genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity. Conceived by UN 1998. Needs state cooperation. (Full detail in Unit 2.)
NATO
North Atlantic Treaty Organization — founded 1949 as a defensive military alliance against Soviet threat. Canada was a founding member. "An attack on one is an attack on all." Role evolved to include peacekeeping/peacemaking after Cold War.
NGOs
Non-governmental organizations — Red Cross, Médecins Sans Frontières, Amnesty International. Provide humanitarian aid independent of governments. Can act quickly in crises.
Benefits of internationalism
Peace & security
UN provides forum for disputes; no WWIII since 1945; peacekeeping missions worldwide
Economic stability
World Bank, IMF, WTO promote trade and financial stability across borders
Humanitarianism
International agencies respond quickly to disasters — 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami response showed value of international cooperation
Indigenous rights
2007 UN Declaration on Rights of Indigenous Peoples — confirmed right to self-determination. Canada, US, Australia, NZ voted against (worried about land/resource clauses).
Globalization's effect on foreign policy
  • Globalization has reduced the role of nation-states and increased the role of multinational corporations and international organizations
  • Domestic and foreign policy are now so intertwined it's hard to separate them — trade rules affect labour laws, environmental policy affects trade
  • Canada combined its foreign affairs and international trade departments in 1982 — reflecting this blurring
Unit 3 · Ch. 10

Peacekeeping & Peacemaking

To what extent can foreign policy promote internationalism?

Canada & peacekeeping origins ★ important
Lester B. Pearson
Canada's minister of external affairs (later PM) who invented modern peacekeeping in 1956 during the Suez Canal crisis. Proposed the UN Emergency Force to defuse a conflict that threatened world war. Won the Nobel Peace Prize.
Canada's record
Since 1956, Canada has taken part in about 50 peacekeeping missions. In 1991, Canada contributed more than 10% of all UN peacekeeping forces. By 2007, this had fallen to less than 0.1%. Some debate whether Canada's "peacekeeping identity" is now more myth than reality.
Peacekeeping rules — the 3 principles
Consent
Must respect the host country's sovereignty
Impartiality
Must not take sides in the conflict
Self-Defence
Can only use force to defend themselves
These rules made peacekeepers ineffective in both Rwanda and Bosnia — they could watch genocide happen but couldn't stop it.
Peacekeeping vs peacemaking
Peacekeeping
Sent in only after a ceasefire. Neutral — can't take sides. Force only in self-defence. Needs host country's consent. Sets up buffer zones, monitors, observes. Not proactive.
Peacemaking
Active military intervention. Not neutral — can take sides. Can shoot to kill. Does not need the country's consent. Goal is to actively end armed conflict and stop human rights abuses. More like war-fighting.
Example of peacemaking
1991 Gulf War — multinational UN force pushed Iraqi invaders out of Kuwait. Used offensive force, not just peacekeeping. This became the model critics wanted after Rwanda's failure.
Peacekeeping failures & the reform debate
Bosnia/Yugoslavia (1990s)
Peacekeepers present but powerless — limited numbers, couldn't use force, genocide continued. The Medak Pocket: Canadian peacekeepers discovered evidence of war crimes but could do nothing.
Rwanda (1994)
General Roméo Dallaire commanded 2600 UN peacekeepers. Warned the UN of genocide risk months before. Asked for more troops and permission to seize Hutu weapons. All requests denied. 800,000 killed in 100 days. UN Secretary-General Boutros Boutros-Ghali later called the inaction "one of the greatest failures of his life."
2004 UN report
"A More Secure World: Our Shared Responsibility" — recommended that the UN be allowed to intervene in internal conflicts when a state fails to protect its own people. Controversial: seen as a direct challenge to national sovereignty.
Peacekeeping debate
Retired Major-General MacKenzie wanted to "get rid of the Canadian myth" of peacekeeping — numbers don't back it up. Others say peacekeeping is part of Canadian national identity and "important in Canadians' self-definition."
Unit 4 · Ch. 13, 14

Canadian National Identity

To what extent have visions of Canadian identity evolved?

Teacher: Ch. 13 applies what we learned in Ch. 1–2 to Canada. Ch. 14 = the forces and institutions that maintain Canadian unity.
What defines Canada as a nation?
  • Civic nation — built on principles in law (Constitution Act 1982, Charter of Rights and Freedoms)
  • Bilingual — French and English both official languages. Official Languages Act (1969) under Trudeau protected French nationally.
  • Multicultural — 1971: first country to adopt multiculturalism as official policy. Canadian Multiculturalism Act (1988) — "mosaic, not melting pot"
  • Federal state — power shared between federal and provincial governments
  • Regional diversity — very strong regional identities. Canada is hard to fit in one box — "muddy and messy" but deliberately inclusive.
  • Constitutional monarchy / parliamentary democracy
National symbols & myths (Ch. 14)
Symbols
Maple leaf (national flag), beaver (national animal — fur trade history, on 5¢ coin), loon (loonie, $1 coin), RCMP uniform (Mounties), hockey
National myths
"Rugged Canadian" triumphing over nature (coureurs de bois, pioneers, building the CPR, playing hockey on outdoor rinks). Canada as peacekeeper. Canadians as funny (Jim Carrey, Mike Myers, etc.). Myths may not be accurate but inspire a shared sense of "who we are."
Debate about myths
Some say myths promote unity. Others say myths exclude groups (who was left out of the "rugged pioneer" myth?). Ernest Renan: a nation is unified by shared memories and the desire to live together. Myths help create those shared memories.
Forces of Canadian unity
Charter of Rights (1982)
Enshrines fundamental rights and freedoms for all Canadians regardless of province
Official Languages Act (1969)
French and English both protected nationally under Trudeau's "two official languages and a pluralist society"
Multiculturalism
Policy celebrating diversity — "Canada has never been a melting pot; more like a tossed salad"
National institutions
CBC (national broadcaster), universal healthcare, national parks — things Canadians share regardless of province
Equalization payments
Federal transfers to poorer provinces to ensure similar public services nationwide — a concrete unifying mechanism
Unit 4 · Ch. 15, 16

Canadian Unity & Tensions

To what extent should Canadian national unity be promoted?

Forces threatening unity
Québec sovereignty
1995 referendum (50.5% NO) was the biggest crisis. Sovereignists vs federalists; Quiet Revolution transformed Québec; Bill 101; Parti Québécois. Ongoing tension about Francophone identity in an English-dominant continent. "It will always be difficult for a small people speaking French in America."
Aboriginal self-determination
First Nations seeking self-government; Indian Act still in force; residential schools; 800+ specific land claims unresolved; AFN gave federal government failing grade (2006). The Oka Crisis (1990) showed how quickly tensions can escalate.
Regionalism
Western alienation (especially Alberta), resource ownership disputes (National Energy Program controversy), provincial vs federal power. Could slide into nationalist loyalty if left unaddressed.
Conscription
WWI and WWII — forced military service divided French and English Canada. 1942 plebiscite: 79% of anglophones supported overseas conscription; 85% of Francophones opposed it.
Multiculturalism debate
Does celebrating diversity strengthen or weaken national unity? Some argue it creates a stronger, more resilient Canada. Others argue it divides Canadians and prevents a shared sense of national identity from developing.
Residential schools & reconciliation
What they were
System designed to forcibly assimilate Indigenous children — removed from families, forbidden to speak their language or practice their culture. Government policy for 150+ years.
Royal Commission (1996)
People to People, Nation to Nation — 66 recommendations. Called the policy "wrong." Recommended self-government, land claim settlements, treaty negotiations. Most recommendations unimplemented by 2006.
Settlements reached
Nisga'a Agreement (1998), Nunavut created (1999), James Bay Agreement (1975). But 800+ claims still unresolved.
National Aboriginal Day
June 21 — one of the few Royal Commission recommendations actually implemented
Reasonable accommodation in Québec
The tension
How can Québec accommodate immigrant cultures while protecting Francophone identity? In 2007, the town of Hérouxville (no immigrants) adopted a code of conduct banning certain immigrant practices — sparked national debate.
Bouchard-Taylor Commission
Québec Premier Charest established this commission to explore reasonable accommodation challenges. Heard testimony across the province about the balance between Francophone identity and cultural diversity.
Unit 1 Supplement · Ch. 2

The French Revolution

A key case study for how nationalism forms — consistently appears on Alberta 20-1 source-based questions.

Your teacher said German nationalism won't appear directly, and the French Revolution isn't a huge focus — but online Alberta 20-1 practice exams consistently test it in source-based questions. Know the key players, events, and what the Revolution demonstrates about nationalism forming.
Social structure — the Old Regime (before 1789)
1st Estate
Catholic Clergy — exempt from taxes, held enormous wealth and power. Represented a tiny minority.
2nd Estate
Nobility — land-owners, exempt from most taxes. Also a small minority.
3rd Estate
Everyone else (~97% of population) — commoners, merchants, peasants. Paid all the taxes. Under the old system, each estate had ONE combined vote, so the 3rd Estate's single vote was always outvoted 2-1.
Source tip: If a source shows pamphlets demanding voting "by head" (one person, one vote) rather than "by order" (one estate, one vote), the authors are almost certainly from the Third Estate — demanding a fairer system where their majority numbers would give them power.
Key events
Estates-General (1789)
King Louis XVI called all three estates to solve France's financial crisis — but it sparked revolution instead. Third Estate demanded voting by head; when refused, they declared themselves the National Assembly.
National Assembly
Third Estate declared itself the National Assembly — claiming to represent the French people, not the king. Radical shift: sovereignty belongs to the nation, not the monarch.
Tennis Court Oath
National Assembly members vowed not to disband until France had a written constitution. Direct challenge to royal absolute power.
Declaration of Rights of Man
1789 — stated equality and freedom for all citizens. Foundational document of modern civic nationalism. Became basis for French national identity.
Reign of Terror
Revolutionary leaders became extreme — thousands executed by guillotine, including the king (Louis XVI) and queen (Marie Antoinette). Shows how nationalism can slide into ultranationalism.
Napoleon
Rose from the Revolution — restored order, spread French ideals of nationalism across Europe through conquest. Paradox: spread the idea of nationhood while conquering other nations and denying them self-determination.
What the French Revolution shows about nationalism
  • Revolution vs evolution: France chose revolution — sudden violent overthrow. The US also chose revolution. Canada chose evolution (gradualism).
  • Civic nationalism forming: The Declaration of Rights of Man was principle-based — "citizen" replaced "subject." Open to anyone who accepted the laws.
  • Economic drivers: Taxes, inequality, and food shortages drove nationalism just as much as ideology. Nationalism doesn't just come from cultural pride — economic grievances fuel it too.
  • Nationalism → ultranationalism: The Reign of Terror shows how quickly revolutionary nationalist movements can become extreme and target their own people.
  • Spreading nationalism: Napoleon's conquests spread the idea of nations and nationalism across Europe — unintentionally fuelling nationalist independence movements everywhere he conquered.
